A Federal College Sports Framework Is Moving

What S.4668 could mean for Michigan athletes and institutions—and which decisions would move, remain or overlap.

Published September 17, 2026Updated September 24, 2026Reading time 9 min

Legislative status

As of September 24, 2026

  • The Senate adopted the Cruz-Cantwell substitute amendment to S.4668 by a 77–23 vote.[1]
  • The Senate then invoked cloture on S.4668, as amended, by a 74–25 vote.[2]
  • The Senate schedule calls for a vote on passage of S.4668, as amended, on September 28.[3]

The bill is not law. Final Senate passage has not yet occurred, and any Senate-passed measure would still require House action before it could become law.

Executive summary

Federal college-sports policy has entered a more consequential phase, and the legislation now under Senate consideration reaches substantially beyond name, image and likeness compensation.

The current proposal addresses athlete agents, transfers, eligibility, scholarships, medical coverage, health and safety, athlete representation, revenue sharing, women's and Olympic sports, recruitment, state-law preemption and sports broadcasting.[4]

For Michigan, the important question is therefore larger than whether Congress eventually passes S.4668. Four questions matter more:

  1. Which decisions would move into a federal framework, and which would remain with the NCAA, conferences and institutions?
  2. How would implementation differ across Michigan's very different athletic institutions?
  3. Where would authority overlap rather than transfer?
  4. Can athletes and families navigate the resulting system before making consequential decisions?

This brief examines each question and closes with the information problem facing athletes and families.

From NIL legislation to a broader governance framework

The proposal would create federal protections for athletes' ability to earn NIL compensation and obtain representation without automatically losing eligibility, scholarships or institutional support. It would expand federal regulation of athlete agents, including registration requirements, a public registry and a proposed five-percent cap on certain agent fees.[5]

NIL is one component among many.

The legislation includes academic protections designed to limit athletic-department interference with academic choices and to protect scholarships from reduction or revocation because of athletic performance, injury, illness or roster-management decisions. Division I institutions would face requirements involving sports-related medical expenses, catastrophic coverage and post-participation medical care. The proposal would establish health and safety standards and create an independent Student Athlete Ombudsman intended to provide confidential guidance and assistance resolving disputes.[6]

Taken together, these provisions describe something closer to a national governing framework for college athletics than a narrow NIL bill.

Transfers and eligibility could become federal policy questions

The proposal would provide athletes a one-transfer protection without loss of eligibility and establish rules governing additional transfers, with specified exceptions involving coaching changes, discontinued sports, graduate study and certain misconduct-related situations.[7] It would also establish baseline eligibility requirements for Division I and Division II athletes, including a five-year eligibility framework with specified exceptions.[8]

For Michigan athletes and families, these provisions could be particularly consequential. Transfer and eligibility decisions are already difficult because the answer depends on the athlete's circumstances, NCAA rules, institutional interpretations and timing. A federal framework could create greater national uniformity in some areas while adding another layer of authority that athletes must understand.

The practical question for an athlete considering a transfer would remain: which rule controls this decision today, and where can an authoritative answer be obtained before acting?

That distinction matters because a rule explained correctly during one season may not remain correct after federal legislation, NCAA implementation or subsequent litigation changes the governing framework.

Revenue sharing would not affect Michigan institutions uniformly

The proposal addresses the emerging revenue-sharing system built around college athletics. The Senate Commerce Committee's summary describes a retention mechanism allowing institutions to spend above the revenue-sharing cap to retain their own athletes, with an additional allowance connected to compensation for athletes in non-revenue-generating sports, including women's and Olympic sports.[9]

A single federal framework produces different implementation pressures depending on division, conference, mix of sponsored sports and available resources. Michigan's NCAA landscape spans nearly the full range of institutional types, which makes the state a useful test case and makes institution-level analysis more informative than asking what the legislation means for “college sports” as a whole.

The last row deserves emphasis. If the framework attaches to NCAA membership, an athlete moving between an NCAA institution and an NAIA or junior-college program may cross into and out of the federal protections entirely. That is exactly the kind of seam where navigation failures occur.

Women's, Olympic and non-revenue sports require implementation-level scrutiny

The proposal contains provisions intended to protect women's, Olympic and other non-revenue-generating sports, including Division I sport minimums and specified roster and grant-in-aid opportunities, while preserving Title IX's applicability.[10]

Supporters argue that a national framework could provide greater financial stability and help preserve athletic opportunities outside football and men's basketball. The NCAA has publicly advocated for passage.[11]

Critics have raised concerns involving the bill's antitrust protections, athlete economic rights, institutional power and how financial benefits and constraints would be distributed. Those concerns have come from labor organizations and civil-rights groups as the Senate debate has intensified.[12]

These competing claims are empirical, and they resolve only after implementation. If a framework is enacted, the important evidence will be what actually happens to roster opportunities, scholarships, sport sponsorship, athlete compensation and institutional spending, disaggregated by sport and by institution type, rather than assuming either the promised protections or the predicted harms will occur uniformly.

Athlete voice and dispute resolution would become more formalized

The proposal would require current or recently former athletes to comprise at least one-third of certain athletic-association governing boards and rulemaking committees. It would also create an independent Student Athlete Ombudsman offering confidential, no-cost guidance and assistance resolving concerns involving institutions, conferences or athletic associations.[13]

Both provisions deserve attention beyond their formal structure. Representation on a governing board does not establish how much influence athletes exercise over decisions. An ombudsman becomes meaningful only if athletes know it exists, understand when to use it and can obtain timely assistance before an irreversible eligibility, contractual or institutional decision occurs.

A formal right and a usable pathway are not the same thing, and the difference is measurable: awareness, time to first contact, time to resolution, and whether the athlete reached the office before or after the decision became irreversible.

State authority would change without disappearing

The proposal would preempt conflicting state and local laws in specified areas involving NIL, transfers and eligibility while preserving other areas of law, including civil rights, privacy, contracts, consumer protection, criminal law and campus safety. It also states that it does not determine whether student athletes are employees or non-employees.[14]

For Michigan, that distinction is important. A federal college-sports framework would not make every athlete-related question exclusively federal. Athletes would continue to encounter layered authority:

FirstFederal law
ThenNCAA or other association rules
ThenConference rules
ThenUniversity policy
ThenContracts
FinallyRemaining state law

Sports wagering introduces a further distinction, because the legal availability of a product to the general public does not establish whether an athlete may participate under eligibility or integrity rules.

The challenge shifts from knowing the rules to knowing which institution has authority over the particular decision being made.

The business structure of college sports is part of the proposal

S.4668 extends into the business structure of college sports. The proposal would create a framework allowing institutions and conferences, subject to specified conditions, to pool certain media rights. It also addresses conference consolidation, use of media rights for sports outside football and basketball, and local broadcast access for football and basketball.[15]

These provisions are particularly relevant to Michigan and Michigan State given their position in the Big Ten's national media market.

They also demonstrate why college-sports policy can no longer be divided into separate conversations about NIL, athlete rights, conference realignment and media. Those systems now interact, and a change in one propagates to the others.

The information problem facing athletes and families

The current debate exposes a larger issue.

An athlete may need to make decisions involving NIL contracts, agents, transfers, eligibility, institutional compensation, sports wagering, healthcare or academic continuation. In a period when the governing framework is itself changing, memorizing a static set of rules is not a safe response.

Recognize the decision
Identify the governing authority
Verify the current rule
Understand the consequence
Obtain qualified assistance when necessary
Document the decision

That sequence is central to MSEEM Forward, Northstar Civic Institute's developing work on athlete mobility and informed decision-making. The purpose is not to tell athletes what to choose. It is to improve their ability to determine who holds authority, what information is reliable and what must be verified before a consequential decision becomes irreversible.

What to watch next

As the legislative process continues, NCI is tracking five questions:

  1. Senate passage. Whether the Senate passes S.4668 as amended and whether any additional changes are made before the scheduled vote.
  2. House pathway. How the House responds to a Senate-passed version and whether the chambers would need to resolve differences.
  3. Differentiation. Which provisions apply differently by division, conference or institutional type, and which institutions fall outside the framework entirely.
  4. Authority. How federal rules would interact with NCAA, conference, institutional and remaining Michigan authority.
  5. Outcomes. If enacted, whether athlete protections, sport-equity provisions and institutional responsibilities produce measurable results in practice.

Michigan needs the capacity to follow a college-sports system in which authority, financial incentives and athlete rights are changing at the same time. Explaining one more piece of legislation is not sufficient.

What NCI will track

Northstar Civic Institute is building an institution-by-institution analysis of how a federal college-sports framework would reach Michigan's athletic programs, organized around the tiers described above. It examines implications for large and small programs, prospective and current athletes, women's and Olympic sports, NIL markets, transfers, eligibility, sports wagering and institutional governance.

If legislation is enacted, the work moves from legislative analysis to implementation measurement:

  • What actually changed for Michigan athletes?
  • Which institutions absorbed new responsibilities, and at what cost relative to their athletic revenue?
  • Which protections became usable in practice rather than only available on paper?
  • Where did athletes continue to experience navigation failures?

Those questions will matter longer than the current floor debate.
