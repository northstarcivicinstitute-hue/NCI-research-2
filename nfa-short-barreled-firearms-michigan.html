Federal Rules for Short-Barreled Rifles Changed. Michigan’s Did Not.

What ATF’s October 9 open letter and Michigan State Police Legal Update No. 173 mean for owners, dealers, officers and prosecutors handling short-barreled rifles and shotguns in Michigan.

Published November 16, 2026Updated November 17, 2026Author Donavan Norman, Executive DirectorStandards Sources & method · CorrectionsReading time 10 min
Public safetyFirearms lawFederal-state implementationCivic Governance Atlas
Research Library
Key takeaways
  • Congress cut the federal making and transfer tax to $0 for short-barreled rifles and shotguns, silencers and “any other weapons,” beginning January 1, 2026. A federal court in Texas then held that the National Firearms Act’s approval and registration provisions are unconstitutional as applied to those untaxed items and barred their enforcement against the plaintiffs and their members and customers. The Justice Department did not appeal.
  • On October 9, 2026, ATF extended that result nationwide as a matter of enforcement policy: it no longer requires approval before those items are made or transferred, or their registration. Filing is now voluntary.
  • Michigan’s law did not change. Michigan State Police Legal Update No. 173 tells officers that a short-barreled rifle or shotgun is still a felony unless it is lawful under federal law, that the person charged must establish that exception, and that the state’s registration and production duties still apply.
  • The practical gap: for a short-barreled rifle or shotgun longer than 26 inches, Michigan requires the possessor to carry and show “a copy of the federal registration.” A federal registration now exists only if the owner files for one.
  • No public data shows how often this arises in Michigan: how many items are seized, returned or forfeited, or how many Michigan residents still file federal forms.
Core finding

A change in federal enforcement is not a change in Michigan law. Michigan’s 2014 statute ties lawful possession to federal law and, for longer short-barreled firearms, to a federal registration document. When federal registration became optional, Michigan’s requirement to produce one did not. The documented way to meet that requirement in Michigan is a federal registration the owner now has to seek voluntarily.

Plain-language terms used in this article
National Firearms Act (NFA)
A 1934 federal law that taxes and registers certain firearms and devices, including machine guns, short-barreled rifles and shotguns, and silencers.
Short-barreled rifle or shotgun
A rifle or shotgun with a barrel, or an overall length, shorter than the legal minimum. Michigan defines both in MCL 750.222.
“Any other weapon”
A federal catch-all category of concealable firearms defined in 26 U.S.C. 5845(e).
Form 1, Form 3, Form 4
ATF applications to make, or to transfer, an NFA firearm. Approval results in registration in the National Firearms Registration and Transfer Record.
Enforcement discretion
An agency’s decision not to enforce a law that remains on the books. It is not a repeal.

Executive summary

For most of a century, owning a short-barreled rifle or shotgun lawfully in the United States meant paying a $200 federal tax, getting federal approval and having the firearm registered. Michigan’s law, as amended in 2014, was built on top of that system: it makes these firearms a felony unless they are “lawfully made, manufactured, transferred, or possessed under federal law,” and it requires owners of the longer ones to carry a copy of the federal registration. [9] [10]

The federal system changed in three steps in 2025 and 2026. Congress set the tax at $0 for these items; a federal court held that, without a tax, the registration rules could not be enforced against the plaintiffs before it; and ATF announced that it would no longer require approval or registration from anyone. [1] [4] [6] ATF’s letter says in so many words that this does “not affect state or local laws that govern NFA weapons,” and the Michigan State Police responded with a legal update to officers saying that, under Michigan law, “nothing has really changed.” [6] [8]

This article sets out what each document says, where federal and state requirements now diverge, and who has to carry out the Michigan rules. It does not interpret federal law for any particular firearm, and it is not legal advice.

What changed federally

DateActionEffect
July 4, 2025Public Law 119-21, sec. 70436 [1]Sets the making and transfer tax at $0 for every NFA firearm except machine guns and destructive devices, which stay at $200, for calendar quarters beginning more than 90 days after enactment: January 1, 2026. [2]
May 8, 2026ATF final rule, 91 FR 25112 [2]Conforms ATF’s tax-remittance regulations, effective June 10, 2026.
Aug. 5, 2026Silencer Shop Foundation v. ATF, N.D. Tex. [3] [4]Holds that “[b]ecause today’s NFA does not generate any revenue from untaxed firearms, its regulatory provisions cannot be upheld under the taxing power,” and permanently enjoins enforcement of the challenged provisions “as to untaxed firearms against the plaintiffs and, where applicable, the plaintiffs’ agencies, political subdivisions, members, and customers.” The judgment was stayed seven days.
Sept. 24, 2026Solicitor General’s letter to Congress [5]States that the Justice Department “has determined not to file an appeal.”
Oct. 9, 2026ATF Director’s open letter [6]“[E]ffective October 9, 2026, ATF will no longer require individuals to seek ATF approval under the NFA before making or transferring short-barreled rifles, short-barreled shotguns, silencers, and ‘any other weapon[s]’ … ATF will also no longer require such items to be registered in the National Firearms Registration and Transfer Record.” The policies “apply uniformly to anyone otherwise subject to these requirements, not just to the plaintiffs in the litigation.”

The open letter keeps the federal forms available: “ATF will continue to administer the NFA on a voluntary basis,” accepting Form 1 applications to make or register these items and Form 3 or Form 4 applications to transfer or register them. [6] It also lists five limits on the change:

  • Special occupational taxes still apply to anyone in the business of manufacturing, importing or dealing in NFA firearms.
  • For machine guns and destructive devices, “all NFA provisions still apply.”
  • Gun Control Act rules still apply: prohibited persons may not possess these items, and recordkeeping and background-check requirements remain.
  • People transporting short-barreled rifles and shotguns across state lines “should continue to file Form 20s, even if the firearms are not registered in the National Firearms Registration and Transfer Record.” ATF adds that “it is unclear whether state courts will apply the safe harbor created by 18 U.S.C. § 926A to interstate transportation of unregistered NFA items.”
  • The injunction and the enforcement policy “do not affect state or local laws that govern NFA weapons.”

The court’s injunction protects the plaintiffs and, where applicable, their agencies, political subdivisions, members and customers, current and future; it does not protect the public at large. The nationwide effect comes from ATF’s enforcement policy, which the agency can revise. The plaintiff states have asked the court to clarify whether the injunction covers everyone within their borders, and the court had not ruled as of November 17. The Justice Department answered on October 12 that clarification was unnecessary because “ATF is not enforcing the challenged NFA provisions in any state.” [4] [7]

What Michigan law still requires

Michigan State Police Legal Update No. 173, issued in October 2026, answers officers’ questions after the open letter. It says the 2014 description in Legal Update No. 106 “continues to present an accurate description of Michigan law,” and declines to say whether any particular firearm is lawful under federal law: “The MSP will not offer an advisory opinion as to whether a particularly described firearm or device is ‘lawfully made, manufactured, transferred, or possessed under federal law.’” [8] [9] The rules it restates come from MCL 750.224b:

SituationWhat Michigan law requiresConsequence
General rule“A person shall not make, manufacture, transfer, or possess a short-barreled shotgun or a short-barreled rifle.” [10]Felony: up to 5 years, a fine up to $2,500, or both.
Federal exceptionThe rule “does not apply to a short-barreled shotgun or short-barreled rifle that is lawfully made, manufactured, transferred, or possessed under federal law.” MCL 776.20 applies to the exception. [10] [11]The person charged must establish the exception. MSP reads this as resting “solely on the defendant”; the statute adds that it “does not shift the burden of proof for the violation.” [8] [11]
26 inches or less in lengthComply with Michigan’s pistol licensing and sales-record provisions, MCL 28.422 and 28.422a. [10] [12] [13]The firearm is treated like a pistol for those purposes.
Greater than 26 inches in lengthCarry “a copy of the federal registration” while transporting or using it, and show it to a peace officer on request. [10]State civil infraction, fine up to $100; immediate seizure; return if the registration is shown within 45 days; otherwise forfeiture under MCL 600.4701 to 600.4709. [8] [10]

The update anticipates the visitor and new-owner situations directly. Officers “should be aware that it may be a true statement that the ATF or certain federal courts have determined that the registration of specific NFA firearms are not currently required under federal law,” but, comparing the situation to a visitor from a state without concealed-carry licensing, MSP concludes that Michigan’s production and pistol-registration duties still apply. It tells officers to “contact their local prosecutor for an interpretation before applying the information.” [8]

Legal Update No. 173 addresses short-barreled rifles and shotguns only. The federal change also covers silencers and “any other weapons”; this article does not analyze how Michigan law treats those items.

Where the two no longer line up

NCI analysis. Three points of friction follow from the documents above. None is resolved by any of them.

  • The registration document. Michigan’s civil-infraction and seizure provisions for longer short-barreled firearms depend on a federal registration existing. After October 9, one exists only if the owner chooses to file. An owner who relies on the federal policy and does not register has nothing to show an officer, and the statute’s 45-day window to produce it does not help.
  • “Lawfully made … under federal law.” Michigan’s felony exception turns on federal lawfulness. Whether an item made or transferred without ATF approval after October 9 meets that test is a question of federal law that MSP declines to answer, that ATF’s letter does not address, and that the court’s injunction answers only for the parties and members it covers. In Michigan it will be decided by prosecutors and, if charged, by courts.
  • Two documents for one firearm. For items 26 inches or less, Michigan’s pistol licensing and sales-record process still applies whatever the federal status. Owners, dealers and officers have to track a federal status that is now optional and a state process that is not.

The Legislature is the body that can change how MCL 750.224b relies on federal registration. NCI takes no position on whether it should.

This is the kind of split authority the Civic Governance Atlas maps: a federal agency changes its enforcement, a federal court defines who is protected, a state statute borrows the federal standard, and local officers and prosecutors carry the result.

What should be measured

NCI found no public data that would show how this works in practice. The records that would answer it already exist in agency files:

  • Seizures under MCL 750.224b(6), how many firearms were returned within 45 days, and how many were forfeited.
  • Charges filed under MCL 750.224b(1), and how many were dismissed on the federal exception.
  • The number of voluntary federal filings by Michigan residents after October 9, and how long they take.
  • Training bulletins or directives issued by local agencies and prosecutors after Legal Update No. 173.

Seizure and charging records are available through public-records requests to individual agencies; NCI has not yet requested them.

A note on processing times

NCI found no ATF announcement changing NFA processing times. The October change is different in kind: for these items, approval is no longer required at all, and the forms remain available for those who want them. [6] Because Michigan’s production requirement depends on a federal registration, how long a voluntary filing takes still matters to Michigan buyers of longer short-barreled firearms. ATF publishes current processing times on its website, but that site was not accessible to NCI’s retrieval tools when this article was prepared, so NCI does not report processing-time figures, including those circulating in secondary sources.

What to watch

  • No date set: The court’s ruling on the state plaintiffs’ motion to clarify the injunction’s scope in Silencer Shop Foundation v. ATF. [7]
  • No date set: Any revision of ATF’s October 9 enforcement policy, which is guidance rather than a rule.
  • No date set: Other federal challenges to the NFA’s registration provisions, including Brown v. ATF (E.D. Mo.), in which ATF’s letter was filed as an exhibit on October 14. [6]
  • No date set: Further Michigan State Police legal updates, and any local prosecutor guidance on the federal exception.
