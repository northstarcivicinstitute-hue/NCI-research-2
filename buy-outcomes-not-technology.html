Surveillance Governance Series · Brief 03

Buy Outcomes, Not Technology

A plain-language procurement framework for agencies buying connected sensors, cloud platforms, analytics and AI-enabled public-safety systems.

Published September 2026Updated September 19, 2026Author Donavan Norman
Public ProcurementPublic Safety TechnologyAI GovernanceCybersecurityMichigan
Download PDFDownload Word fileAll briefs
Core recommendation

Public-safety procurement should begin with a lawful public purpose and a measurable need, then apply scrutiny proportional to risk. The contract should preserve government control over data, security, evaluation, material changes, continuity, and exit. A scorecard can organize judgment, but legal authority, basic security, auditability, and data control are non-negotiable conditions.

Plain-language terms used in this brief
ALPR
Automatic license plate reader: a camera system that records plate images and related time and location data.
High-impact system
A system that can materially affect rights, liberty, access, enforcement decisions or persistent monitoring.
Supplier provenance
Who owns, develops, hosts and supports a product and where its critical components come from.
Living system plan
A record of the system’s purpose, data, controls, suppliers, changes, incidents and owners that is updated throughout the contract.

What changed in the 2026 guidance

The procurement environment has moved beyond general calls for responsible technology. NIST finalized two directly usable system and supplier resources in summer 2026. SP 800-18 Rev. 2 treats security, privacy, and cybersecurity supply-chain plans as connected system plans and emphasizes purpose, data flows, components, roles, controls, and lifecycle decisions. SP 1326 provides a quick-start structure for supplier due diligence. [3-4]

NIST also released an initial public draft of the TEVV-Athlon framework in August 2026. It is designed to structure test, evaluation, verification, and validation of real-world AI systems and is open for comment through October 6. The existing AI Risk Management Framework remains available, but NIST states that AI RMF 1.0 is being revised. [6-7]

Strategic implication

Public agencies now have stronger building blocks for turning procurement review into an evidence record: define the system boundary, map the data and decision flow, examine the supplier, test the actual use case, control changes after award, and require results at renewal.

Michigan is scaling capability while statewide rules remain incomplete

Michigan's ALPR proposals remain pending rather than enacted statewide law. HB 5492 and HB 5493 were introduced on January 29, 2026, and referred to the House Judiciary Committee. SB 1131 was introduced on July 29 and referred to the Senate Civil Rights, Judiciary, and Public Safety Committee. The bills take different approaches to governmental and private systems, retention, access, reporting, remedies, and permitted use. [9-11]

Contemporaneous reporting in April 2026 identified ALPR use across more than 125 Michigan cities and counties. Because statewide legislation remains pending, local policy and contract terms continue to do much of the practical governance work. [15]

The same implementation question is emerging in advanced air mobility. In August 2026, Michigan awarded $2 million for five pilots pairing companies with EGLE, DNR, MDOT, MSP, and MDARD for environmental monitoring, mapping and wildfire support, roadway intelligence, drone detection, and pesticide-drift investigation. The state's 2025 AAM report described more than $42 million in public and private investment and nine active test sites. On September 17, 2026, the state announced another $2 million for 26 mobility, defense, aerospace, and manufacturing companies and reported more than $5 million awarded to 69 firms across three rounds. [12-14]

These investments are not proof that any specific system should be purchased. They increase the need for repeatable supplier, system, contract, and outcome controls as pilots become operational programs.

Federal ALPR policy now adds another governance layer

H.R. 10366, the No Federal License-Plate Observation and Camera Keeping Act, was introduced in the U.S. House on September 14, 2026 and referred to the Committee on Transportation and Infrastructure. The proposal would condition apportioned surface-transportation funding on states adopting specified restrictions for automated license-plate-reader use. It remains pending legislation and does not create a current federal mandate for Michigan agencies. [16]

Implementation consideration: Agencies evaluating procurement resilience can examine whether retention, permitted-use, sharing and deletion rules can be changed without replacing the entire system if federal or state requirements later tighten.

The relevant contract question is whether legal or policy changes can be implemented without forcing a full system replacement.

Four conditions that must be met before purchase

GateQuestion that must be answered before scoringPause condition
Lawful authority and public purposeWhat legal authority and defined public-safety purpose support the intended use?Purpose is vague, authority is missing, or the use would violate law or constitutional protections.
Minimum controlCan the government control access, logging, retention, sharing, security, audit, and deletion?Sensitive data or consequential functions lack basic controls or cannot be independently audited.
Evidence accessCan the agency obtain enough information to test performance, investigate errors, and disclose material limitations?Vendor terms or system design prevent meaningful evaluation, error investigation, or oversight.
Exit and continuityCan the agency export data, transition operations, delete information, and withstand supplier or product disruption?No credible exit, deletion, transition, or continuity path exists.

A high numerical score cannot cure a failed gate. This is especially important for biometric, persistent-surveillance, integrated-data, and consequential AI systems, where one missing control can dominate the risk.

Eight steps from problem definition to exit

  1. Define the problem and baseline. State the public-safety outcome, current performance, affected population or geography, and credible non-technology alternatives.
  2. Set the risk tier. Consider data sensitivity, persistence, automation, rights impact, integration, scale, and dependence on a supplier.
  3. Build the system plan. Document purpose, system boundary, components, data flows, roles, controls, and lifecycle using SP 800-18 Rev. 2 as a practical model.
  4. Review evidence and supplier. Separate vendor claims from independent evidence and conduct proportionate supplier due diligence using SP 1326.
  5. Test the real use case. Establish test data, operating conditions, success criteria, error analysis, human review, and comparison points; use TEVV concepts for AI-enabled functions.
  6. Contract for performance and control. Resolve data rights, cybersecurity, audit, change notice, evaluation, continuity, portability, termination, and deletion before deployment.
  7. Monitor deployment drift. Track model, software, data-source, integration, workflow, staffing, and use-policy changes after award.
  8. Make renewal affirmative. Report outcomes, total cost, incidents, changes, risks, and alternatives on a fixed schedule, then continue, modify, narrow, expand, or exit.

NIJ's 2025 adoption guide reinforces the same basic order: problem analysis first, followed by technical, operational, and governance review. [1]

Match review intensity to risk

TierTypical characteristicsReview expectation
Tier 1 - routineLow sensitivity, limited personal data, minimal integration, and little effect on rights or operational decisions.Streamlined need, security, supplier, cost, and records review.
Tier 2 - significantSensitive data, meaningful integration or workflow change, material vendor dependence, or moderate rights and privacy impact.Full impact assessment, legal/privacy/data review, system plan, measurable implementation plan, and formal renewal.
Tier 3 - high impactBiometrics, persistent surveillance, consequential AI or automation, broad data fusion, or systems likely to materially affect liberty, rights, or enforcement.Enhanced validation, senior approval, independent or cross-functional review, time-limited pilot where feasible, public use policy, and stronger audit/change/exit controls.

Risk tiering should affect process, not predetermine the decision. A Tier 3 system may be appropriate when authority, need, evidence, controls, and outcomes support it. A Tier 1 tool can still fail if the vendor, contract, or security posture is weak.

How to read the score

Scores expose gaps and document the record. They do not manufacture certainty. A failed non-negotiable condition blocks procurement regardless of total points. Significant weakness should lead to cure, narrower scope, a pilot, a different supplier, or no award.

Review the supplier, not only the product

NIST SP 1326 organizes supplier due diligence around five components: foreign ownership, control, or influence; provenance; resilience; foundational cybersecurity practices; and supply-chain tiers. The guide is scoped to information and communications technology suppliers, which fits many connected sensors, cloud platforms, analytics products, and AI-enabled systems used in public safety. [4]

Supplier questionEvidence to request
Who ultimately owns or controls the supplier and material subcontractors?Corporate structure, controlling interests, locations, sanctions or legal restrictions, and notification of change of control.
Where did critical hardware, software, models, and data components originate?Bill of materials or equivalent provenance information appropriate to risk; development and hosting locations; critical dependencies.
Can the service withstand disruption?Business continuity, disaster recovery, redundancy, financial stability, product-lifecycle plans, and subcontractor substitution.
Are foundational cyber practices credible?Security program, vulnerability management, independent assessments, incident history, patching, logging, and notification commitments.
How deep is the supply chain?Material vendors, cloud and model providers, data sources, integrators, support firms, and flow-down contract obligations.

Create a living system plan, not a one-time form

NIST SP 800-18 Rev. 2 links security, privacy, and supply-chain planning. It calls for documentation of the system purpose, components, environments, data flows, controls, roles, and information lifecycle. It also emphasizes machine-readable formats and continuous risk information rather than static point-in-time paperwork. [3]

  • System boundary and intended tasks, including integrations and external services.
  • Data created, collected, inferred, received, shared, stored, exported, and deleted.
  • Named owners for operations, security, privacy, legal compliance, records, evaluation, and contract management.
  • Controls in place, planned controls, known gaps, exceptions, and target dates.
  • Supplier and subcontractor dependencies, hosting, model providers, and critical support services.
  • Version, change, incident, complaint, and audit history throughout the contract.

The federal Privacy Impact Assessment model is also useful procedurally even when it is not legally required of a local agency. It asks how identifiable information is collected, stored, protected, shared, and managed across the lifecycle. [8]

Control model and capability changes after award

AI features can appear in ALPR search, video analytics, weapons detection, identity matching, report generation, investigative search, prioritization, and decision support. The version demonstrated during procurement may not be the version operating months later. Contracts and system plans should distinguish routine maintenance from a material capability change.

ChangeRequired response
Model, algorithm, threshold, or training-data changeAdvance notice; updated validation; error and subgroup analysis appropriate to the use; approval before production when material.
New feature or taskNew purpose and authority review; updated risk tier, system plan, user training, and public-use policy.
New data source or integrationUpdated data map, privacy/security review, retention/sharing terms, provenance, and access controls.
Vendor or subcontractor changeSupplier due diligence, contract flow-down, continuity analysis, and data-location review.
Observed deployment driftCorrective action, retraining or rollback, scope limitation, user guidance, and documented reauthorization.

NIST's TEVV-Athlon draft offers a structured way to think about real-world AI testing and evaluation. It is a draft, not a binding standard, but procurement teams can already use its core lesson: test the system in the actual task, context, and operating environment rather than relying only on a general benchmark. [7]

NIST Privacy Framework 1.1 should also be cited accurately. As of this update, the final 1.1 version is still listed as forthcoming; the initial public draft remains the available update. [5]

Minimum contract controls

Contract areaQuestion that must be answered before signature
PerformanceWhat service levels and outcome reports distinguish uptime from public-safety value?
DataWho controls data; where are they stored; who may access, share, combine, retain, export, and delete them?
Secondary use and model trainingMay agency data be used for analytics, product development, or training? Under what approval and de-identification rules?
Security and incidentsWhat baseline, vulnerability, logging, notification, remediation, insurance, and subcontractor duties apply?
Audit and evaluationWhat records can the government, oversight bodies, and qualified evaluators access, and can findings be published without vendor veto?
Material changesWhich algorithm, model, feature, integration, data-practice, hosting, ownership, or price changes require notice and approval?
Records and legal processHow will the vendor support FOIA, discovery, litigation holds, evidence preservation, chain of custody, and legally required disclosure?
Continuity and grant cliffWhat happens if the product is discontinued, a subcontractor fails, funding ends, price changes materially, or service is unavailable?
ExitHow will termination, transition, export, deletion, hardware removal, and post-contract security be verified?

Cross-functional review should not diffuse accountability. Each required decision needs a named owner, a written recommendation, and an identified approving official.

A 90-day implementation path for Michigan agencies

  1. Adopt a two-page Public Safety Technology Impact Assessment before an RFP, pilot, grant commitment, or sole-source purchase.
  2. Classify systems into routine, significant, and high-impact tiers and publish the triggers for each tier.
  3. Create a system-plan template aligned to SP 800-18 Rev. 2 and a supplier checklist aligned to SP 1326.
  4. Publish model clauses for data control, security, audit, model change, records, continuity, portability, deletion, and exit.
  5. Require a time-limited pilot and defined comparison when evidence is weak or the use is high-impact and a pilot is feasible.
  6. Maintain a public inventory of covered systems, purposes, vendors, contract dates, policies, and evaluation schedules, with lawful security redactions.
  7. Make renewal an affirmative written decision explaining outcomes, total cost, incidents, changes, residual risk, alternatives, and exit readiness.

The pending ALPR bills provide a useful test case. Agencies can map the proposed statutory safeguards against their own policies and contracts now, identify which controls require statewide law, and implement the remaining controls through procurement before legislation is resolved.

Bottom line

The key procurement question is not whether a product is innovative. It is whether the public agency has lawful authority, a defined need, credible evidence, operational capacity, data and security control, a resilient supplier, an evaluable contract, and an exit path. The 2026 NIST updates make that record easier to build. Michigan's growing ALPR and advanced-mobility activity makes it more urgent to use it.
