AI & Automated Decisions

Verified developments in how Michigan and federal agencies buy, deploy, test, correct, and retire AI and automated decision systems.

Federal AI policyOct. 2

Executive orders set up America.gov and change federal AI terminology

Fact. Executive Order 14432, signed September 29 and published October 2, directs GSA, working with the National Design Studio and OMB, to establish America.gov as a single, conversational point of entry for federal services that serve more than 100,000 people a year and can be used online, with Login.gov as its sign-in; the site launched September 29. IRS tax filing, the Department of War and the intelligence community are excluded. Agencies must identify covered services, connect them through existing APIs, dashboards and forms, and report usage and performance data; OMB must issue an implementation memo within 90 days. The order says agencies keep custody of their records and decision authority, that no centralized federal system of records is created, and that in-person, phone and mail options remain. A separate order, EO 14434, directs agencies to use “Super Intelligence” (SI) in place of “artificial intelligence” in non-statutory documents; the statutory definition is unchanged.

NCI analysis. America.gov applies the Atlas questions to a public-service front door: who answers residents, how accuracy is checked, how errors are corrected and logged, and whether people keep a non-digital route. Because the terminology order changes wording rather than the statutory definition, NCI will track federal documents under either term.

Public-sector procurementApplies Oct. 19

GSA sets data-safeguarding terms for AI contracts

Fact. GSA issued a new contract clause, GSAR 552.239-7001, “Basic Safeguarding of Data within Large Language Model AI Systems,” in memo RGO-2026-01. It applies to GSA solicitations issued on or after October 19, 2026 that buy large language model, chatbot, generative-assistant, agentic or other LLM-enabled tools, and contracting officers may add it to existing contracts. Contractors may not use government data to train or fine-tune models, for advertising or for sale or licensing; must report material violations and security incidents within 72 hours; must disclose which LLMs they use; and must flow the requirements down to AI subcontractors.

NCI analysis. These are the data and accountability terms the Atlas asks buyers to define at the point of purchase. State and local agencies buying similar tools can compare their own contract language against this federal baseline.

Surveillance & Sensor Governance

License plate readers and other public-safety sensor technology: adoption, policies, retention, audits, data sharing, and contract decisions.

Municipal governanceOct. 6

Detroit approves park cameras with retention and use limits

Fact. On October 6, Detroit City Council approved a three-year, $2.7 million contract with Bayview Electric Company for video surveillance systems, network connectivity and new lighting in Chandler, Eliza-Howell, Maheras-Gentry and Rouge parks. The contract was reviewed under the city’s Community Input Over Government Surveillance ordinance. As reported, video will be stored on the city’s own system and kept up to 30 days unless it contains criminal evidence, the cameras will connect to the Detroit Police Department’s Real Time Crime Center, and the data will not be used for traffic or immigration enforcement. Council also required the General Services Department to post its answers and contracts online and to send quarterly reports on incidents and complaints. Council Member Gabriela Santiago-Romero cast the only no vote (BridgeDetroit reporting).

NCI analysis. Detroit’s ordinance produced the written record the Atlas asks for before deployment: collection scope, retention, sharing, prohibited uses and reporting. The quarterly reports will show whether those terms hold in practice.

Municipal governanceOct. 2–5

Milford ends its Flock contract; Oakland County investigates a former deputy’s searches

Fact. On October 5, the Milford Village Council voted 7-0 not to renew its contract for the village’s remaining 10 Flock Safety license-plate cameras, which expires later this month; the village ended a separate five-camera contract in June. Police Chief Scott Tarasiewicz said the cameras had helped solve crimes but cited downed and vandalized cameras and the vendor’s responsiveness, and residents had raised privacy concerns (Local 4 reporting). Separately, the Oakland County Sheriff’s Office confirmed it is investigating a former deputy for allegedly accessing the county’s Flock system after leaving county employment, and is reviewing potential criminal charges (CBS Detroit reporting). The Detroit News, citing audit logs, reported that the searches included immigration-related queries; a sheriff’s spokesperson said the queries were unauthorized and violated policy. No charges have been filed.

NCI analysis. The two cases test different controls. Milford’s is a contract and service-quality decision. Oakland County’s turns on how system access is revoked when employment ends and how often audit logs are reviewed.

Federal legislationSept. 30–Oct. 2

Two federal proposals would restrict license-plate readers

Fact. Rep. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez introduced H.R. 10691, the Ban Flock Act, on October 1, and Sens. Bernie Sanders and Jeff Merkley released Senate text on October 2. It would bar federal agencies from acquiring or using automated license-plate readers or their data without explicit authorization from Congress, withhold grants from certain federal agencies from state and local governments that use the readers, and bar federal funds for buying them. On September 30, Sen. Josh Hawley said he will introduce a Stop Flock Abuse Act that would instead set conditions on use: written approval for each search, audit logs, deletion after 10 days except for active criminal investigations, and no sale or sharing of data with non-governmental parties. Neither proposal is law.

NCI analysis. The proposals frame the same choice Michigan’s HB 6411-6413 and HB 6346 raise at the state level: prohibit the technology, or regulate its use through search authorization, retention limits, audits and sharing rules. Federal grant conditions could also bear on local contract decisions.

CourtsOct. 1

Federal court suppresses evidence from a warrantless license-plate-reader search

Fact. On October 1, U.S. District Judge Sara E. Hill of the Northern District of Oklahoma granted a defendant’s motion to suppress in United States v. Kyle (No. 4:26-cr-00189). The court found that a Tulsa County deputy’s queries of two license-plate-reader systems, Flock and VehicleManager, for the defendant’s historical location information were a Fourth Amendment search under Carpenter v. United States, made without a warrant or probable cause, and suppressed the evidence that followed. The ruling is from a single federal trial court outside Michigan and does not bind Michigan courts.

NCI analysis. If other courts adopt this reasoning, searches of stored plate data would need the same legal footing as other location-data requests. Agency policies on when a query requires a warrant, and how the reason for each query is recorded, would carry more weight.

Data Centers & Digital Infrastructure

Local and state rules on siting, energy and water use, public costs, and community notice for data centers and related infrastructure.

Local zoningOct. 5–15

Kalamazoo writes its data-center moratorium into zoning; Ypsilanti takes comment on draft rules

Fact. On October 5, the Kalamazoo City Commission unanimously approved a zoning ordinance amendment that formalizes the city’s moratorium on data centers, battery energy storage systems, cryptocurrency mining centers and AI data centers until October 5, 2027. It takes effect 10 days after approval, and permanent rules would replace it if adopted sooner (Watershed Voice reporting). The City of Ypsilanti is holding public meetings on a proposed ordinance that would set regulations for data centers within city limits, in person on October 7 and on Zoom at 10 a.m. on October 15. As presented September 15, the draft would sort data centers into three size categories and set energy and water requirements (Eastern Echo reporting).

NCI analysis. The two cities show the main local paths: pause and then write rules, as Kalamazoo is doing, or write rules first, as Ypsilanti and Saginaw are. Comparing their notice, disclosure and resource-use requirements once adopted will be more useful than counting moratoriums.

Grid & interconnectionOct. 2

MISO asks FERC to approve a fast-track study for large loads paired with new generation

Fact. The Midcontinent Independent System Operator (MISO), which manages the high-voltage grid across most of Michigan, asked the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission on October 2 to approve a Large Load Addition Resource Study (Docket ER27-25). The 120-day study would cover new loads above 200 MW paired with new generation in the same local resource zone; the load and generation must be in service within three years of the application, and MISO would study up to 10 applications in each of three annual study periods. MISO asked FERC to decide by December 2 (Utility Dive reporting). The proposal is pending.

NCI analysis. A faster federal interconnection path could shorten the time between a project’s announcement and its utility commitments. Local zoning, notice and community-benefit processes in Michigan would need to keep pace with that timeline.

Utility regulationOct. 1

State regulators approve DTE’s Google data-center contracts with cost protections

Fact. On October 1, the Michigan Public Service Commission approved special contracts between DTE Electric and Google for electric service to a data center in Van Buren Township, Wayne County (Case No. U-22058). Terms include a 20-year contract, compared with the 5 years normally required under DTE’s large-load D11 rate; an 80% minimum billing demand, compared with 50%-65% under D11; an early-termination payment of at least 15 years of minimum monthly charges; and credit and collateral requirements. Google is to pay for DTE to develop up to 1,600 MW of renewable energy and 480 MW of battery storage. The commission says its authority covers only the contracts’ terms, not whether or where a data center may locate. The same day, the Wayne County Commission overrode County Executive Warren Evans’s veto of tax incentives and a community benefits agreement for the project; the commission said the vote did not approve construction.

NCI analysis. The order gives the Atlas a documented Michigan benchmark for cost allocation: contract length, minimum bills, exit payments and collateral. Siting, tax incentives and community benefits sit with township and county bodies, so the project’s full public record is split across several venues.

Saginaw adopts data-center zoning rules before any project arrives

Fact. Saginaw City Council approved a data-center ordinance on September 28; it is expected to be added to the city code on October 9 (WNEM reporting). Prediction-market jurisdictionOct. 7

Coinbase agrees to halt sports event contracts in Michigan by Oct. 10

Fact. The Michigan Gaming Control Board announced October 7 that Coinbase Financial Markets has agreed, in a stipulation with the state, to stop offering new sports-related event contracts to Michigan customers, including those traded on KalshiEX and other designated contract markets, and to close out open customer positions by 12:00 a.m. Eastern on October 10. The state agreed not to take enforcement action while Coinbase complies and related Sixth Circuit appeals proceed. A federal court denied Coinbase a preliminary injunction in August. The agreement does not resolve whether Michigan may regulate CFTC-registered event contracts as gambling. NCI analysis. For Michigan youth-exposure work, the practical effect is fewer platforms offering sports event contracts to Michigan residents outside the state’s licensing system while the legal question stays open. The October 2 Illinois ruling shows that courts in another circuit can reach the opposite answer, so the arrangement may not be final.

Prediction-market jurisdictionOct. 2

Federal judge blocks parts of Illinois law for sports event contracts

Fact. On October 2, U.S. District Judge Martha M. Pacold of the Northern District of Illinois granted in part, and continued in part, preliminary-injunction motions brought against Illinois officials by Coinbase, by the United States and the CFTC, and by Kalshi. The opinion concludes that many of the sports event contracts at issue are likely swaps under the Commodity Exchange Act, and that Illinois’s sports-wagering licensing requirements and related criminal provisions are likely preempted as applied to them. Whether Illinois’s transaction fees on exchange wagers are preempted was left for further briefing. In late September, the Sixth Circuit, whose rulings bind federal courts in Michigan, ruled against Kalshi in cases involving Ohio and Tennessee (The Block reporting). Neither ruling is a final judgment.

NCI analysis. Who regulates sports event contracts determines who can set age, advertising and responsible-gaming rules for them. With federal courts divided, the answer for Michigan youth-exposure work may depend on the circuit until a higher court resolves it.

New law requires an annual parent notice on youth THC, vaping and nicotine risks

Federal education policyOct. 2

Treasury proposes rules for the federal scholarship tax credit

Fact. Treasury and the IRS published proposed and temporary regulations on October 2 for the new federal tax credit for contributions to scholarship-granting organizations, which is available only in states that elect to participate. The credit is capped at $1,700 per taxpayer, which Treasury interprets as up to $3,400 on a joint return. For 2027, a state must submit its advance election by January 1, 2027, and may complete it by filing its list of scholarship organizations by February 15, 2027. Michigan does not appear on the IRS list of participating states. Comments on the proposed rules are due December 1, and a public hearing is scheduled for December 15.

NCI analysis. Whether Michigan participates is a decision for state officials. NCI’s interest is in what any decision would require: which office certifies organizations, what data are reported and how outcomes could be evaluated.

AccountabilitySept. 30

Auditor General finds gaps in oversight of partnership-district grants

Fact. A Michigan Office of the Auditor General performance audit of MDE’s Office of Partnership Districts (Report No. 313-0201-26) found that from 2022-23 through 2024-25 the office disbursed $42.4 million in Section 21(h) grants to 45 partnership districts with 99 low-performing schools. The audit rated the office’s support efforts sufficient with exceptions and its monitoring of grant spending not sufficient. As a material condition, it found the office did not conduct required annual fiscal risk assessments or review any evidence supporting expenditures of the grants. Two reportable conditions found that 43% of required progress meetings were not documented and that the office overpaid staff by 30% on delayed and erroneous travel reimbursement requests. MDE agreed with all three findings.

NCI analysis. The audit separates whether support reached schools from whether grant spending was verified. For NCI’s accountability work, the governance question is what evidence a state program must collect to show that public funds produced the intended result.
