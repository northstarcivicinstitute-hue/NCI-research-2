AI & Automated Decisions

Verified developments in how Michigan and federal agencies buy, deploy, test, correct, and retire AI and automated decision systems.

AI consumer protectionOct. 14–23

Michigan joins a states’ objection to the Character.AI sale terms

Fact. Attorney General Dana Nessel announced on October 15 that Michigan had joined a coalition of attorneys general from 39 states and the District of Columbia in a filing in the bankruptcy of Character Technologies, Inc., the company behind the Character.AI chatbot platform (In re Character Technologies, No. 26-11603, U.S. Bankruptcy Court for the District of Delaware). The company filed for Chapter 11 on October 12. The states’ notice, docketed October 14, says the company’s records show more than 21.6 million U.S. accounts created by March 13, 2025, including more than 2.1 million with a declared age under 18. It asks that any buyer adopt protections including age assurance that does not rely only on self-declared birthdates, removal of characters presenting as therapists, doctors or other licensed professionals, crisis-response protocols, and no use of chat content to train models or for advertising without valid consent, and it reserves the states’ right to seek a consumer privacy ombudsman and to object to any sale. Court filings name Disney Worldwide Services as the proposed lead bidder for certain assets at $20 million. On October 23 a coalition of 31 states and the District of Columbia, Michigan’s attorney general among them, filed a formal objection to the company’s bidding procedures. The states ask the court to deny the request or attach conditions. They argue that approving the procedures as written would ratify releases the company gave before it filed for bankruptcy, including a release of an insider, before any creditors’ committee exists to examine them; that the proposed $700,000 in bid protections for Disney is not justified because Disney obtained the workforce, the releases and exclusivity before the filing; that the minimum overbid is set high enough to discourage competing bids; that the sale timetable follows milestones Disney set before the case began; and that the notice sent to people with injury claims does not tell them the sale order would permanently bar them from suing Disney’s new employees, including the former chief executive, with silence treated as consent. The court had not ruled as of October 28, and the hearing remains set for October 30.

NCI analysis. A bankruptcy sale is one of the few points where regulators can attach conditions to an AI system’s data and design without a new law, because the buyer acquires the users’ conversations along with the code. The states’ list amounts to a working definition of the safeguards an AI companion service should carry — age assurance, limits on professional personas, crisis protocols and no secondary use of chat data — and the October 30 hearing will show whether the court treats any of it as a condition of sale. The objection adds a second question: whether the people who say the product harmed them keep the right to sue, or trade it away by not responding to a notice.

Federal procurementOct. 20

Federal civilian agencies ordered to exclude and remove DeepSeek AI products

Fact. The Department of Homeland Security published notice on October 20 of an order by the Secretary of Homeland Security under the Federal Acquisition Supply Chain Security Act of 2018. The order excludes information-technology products and services developed by Hangzhou DeepSeek Artificial Intelligence Co. and its affiliates from federal civilian agency procurements, and requires removal of DeepSeek and affiliate products “and third-party products and services developed using DeepSeek implementations” from civilian agency information technology. The signed order is reproduced in the notice and filed in DHS Docket DHS-2026-0628. It applies to federal agencies, not to state or local governments.

NCI analysis. The order reaches past the vendor to products built on its model, which means an agency can comply only if it knows which AI models sit inside the software it buys. Few state or local contracts require vendors to disclose that. For Michigan agencies and districts, the Atlas question is whether procurement terms would let them find and remove a model if a federal or state order, a security finding or a contract dispute required it.

Automated decisionsOct. 12

Michigan joins a suit over cancelled unemployment-system modernization grants

Fact. Attorney General Dana Nessel announced on October 12 that Michigan and 13 other states filed suit in the U.S. Court of Federal Claims the week before, challenging the U.S. Department of Labor’s May 22, 2025 termination of more than $45 million in the plaintiffs’ remaining grant funding for unemployment-insurance technology and fraud prevention. Congress created the grants in the American Rescue Plan Act of 2021 and the department awarded states more than $780 million across six programs: IT modernization, integrity, navigator, tiger team, equity and fraud prevention. Michigan’s awards were in two of those categories: $6.78 million in equity grants, of which $2.83 million was rescinded, and $4.47 million in tiger-team grants, of which $1.41 million was rescinded. The termination letters said the agreements “no longer effectuate [the Department of Labor’s] priorities for its grant funding.” The states argue the terminations breached the grant agreements as contracts and the implied duty of good faith and fair dealing, and they seek money damages. The court docket sets the government’s answer for December 8, and a Justice Department lawyer appeared for the United States on October 14.

NCI analysis. The two categories Michigan lost money from are the ones aimed at access barriers, payment timeliness and fairness in fraud prevention — the same ground as the Atlas’s MiDAS case, in which automated fraud findings were the failure. Because the suit asks for damages rather than reinstated funding, it will not change the November 2 and November 9 cutover dates. What it can produce is a record of which approved Michigan projects stopped mid-performance and what the state did instead, which the release does not identify.

Automated decisionsOct. 26; Nov. 2–9

State sets the November 2 MiDAS shutdown and the November 9 MiUI launch

Fact. Michigan’s Unemployment Insurance Agency, in its October employer newsletter, says the MiDAS/MiWAM platform will be taken offline permanently at 4:30 p.m. on November 2. A downtime period follows until MiUI for benefits launches at 8 a.m. on November 9; during it, UIA local offices will be closed and its customer-service and employer-ombudsman phone lines will be unavailable. Employers must activate their own MiUI accounts before MiWAM shuts down; a third-party administrator cannot do it for them. The same newsletter also gives November 9 as the date MiWAM for employers goes offline. Around October 15 the agency posted claimant guidance for the switch: November 2 is the final MiWAM certification day, open to any claimant from 8 a.m. to 4:30 p.m. regardless of assigned day; at 4:30 p.m. the MARVIN phone line shuts down for good, ending certification and claim filing by phone; and fact-finding responses and protests or appeals that fall due during the downtime become due November 9. After launch, claimants can certify for past weeks, including the transition week, and MiUI moves certification and payment from every two weeks to weekly. A public launch release on October 26 confirms those dates and states that MiWAM and MARVIN are permanently discontinued, that filing a new claim by phone ends, and that local offices stay open with self-serve kiosks, language assistance and customer-service staff. During the downtime the agency still receives mail and faxes, and its website and chatbot remain available.

NCI analysis. This sets the end date of the MiDAS system the Atlas uses as its Automated Decisions case. Moving deadlines that fall inside the downtime to November 9 and allowing back-certification are the kind of error-correction safeguards the Atlas looks for; ending phone filing removes a channel some claimants rely on. The records to watch are whether payments for the transition week arrive without gaps and how many appeals are filed in the first weeks of MiUI.

Federal AI policyOct. 2

Executive orders set up America.gov and change federal AI terminology

Fact. Executive Order 14432, signed September 29 and published October 2, directs GSA, working with the National Design Studio and OMB, to establish America.gov as a single, conversational point of entry for federal services that serve more than 100,000 people a year and can be used online, with Login.gov as its sign-in; the site launched September 29. IRS tax filing, the Department of War and the intelligence community are excluded. Agencies must identify covered services, connect them through existing APIs, dashboards and forms, and report usage and performance data; OMB must issue an implementation memo within 90 days. The order says agencies keep custody of their records and decision authority, that no centralized federal system of records is created, and that in-person, phone and mail options remain. A separate order, EO 14434, directs agencies to use “Super Intelligence” (SI) in place of “artificial intelligence” in non-statutory documents; the statutory definition is unchanged.

NCI analysis. America.gov applies the Atlas questions to a public-service front door: who answers residents, how accuracy is checked, how errors are corrected and logged, and whether people keep a non-digital route. Because the terminology order changes wording rather than the statutory definition, NCI will track federal documents under either term.

Public-sector procurementApplies Oct. 19

GSA sets data-safeguarding terms for AI contracts

Fact. GSA issued a new contract clause, GSAR 552.239-7001, “Basic Safeguarding of Data within Large Language Model AI Systems,” in memo RGO-2026-01. It applies to GSA solicitations issued on or after October 19, 2026 that buy large language model, chatbot, generative-assistant, agentic or other LLM-enabled tools, and contracting officers may add it to existing contracts. Contractors may not use government data to train or fine-tune models, for advertising or for sale or licensing; must report material violations and security incidents within 72 hours; must disclose which LLMs they use; and must flow the requirements down to AI subcontractors.

NCI analysis. These are the data and accountability terms the Atlas asks buyers to define at the point of purchase. State and local agencies buying similar tools can compare their own contract language against this federal baseline.

Federal legislationSept. 30–Oct. 10

Senate bill text would require agencies to inventory, disclose and offer human review of AI decisions

Fact. Sen. Gary Peters of Michigan and Sen. James Lankford of Oklahoma introduced S.5648 on September 30, a bill “to direct agencies to be transparent when using automated and augmented systems to interact with the public or make critical decisions.” It was referred to the Senate Homeland Security and Governmental Affairs Committee. The introduced text, posted October 10, names it the Transparent Automated Governance Act. Within 180 days of enactment, each agency would publish an inventory of the decision processes in which AI substantially influences access to benefits, housing, employment, education, health care and other “critical decisions.” Guidance from the Office of Management and Budget would require plain-language notice that such a process was used, an appeals process with a deadline, an option for review by a human independent of the automated process, and published error metrics; chatbots and voice agents would have to disclose themselves, offer escalation to a person and provide a transcript within three business days on request. It is not law.

NCI analysis. Requiring agencies to disclose when an automated system deals with the public or shapes a critical decision puts the Atlas “Decision” question into a bill. The text answers the Atlas’s questions about notice, appeal and correction in statute rather than guidance, and its human-review option is the safeguard whose absence defined Michigan’s MiDAS failure. It applies to federal agencies only, so state systems such as MiUI would not be covered.

Surveillance & Sensor Governance

License plate readers and other public-safety sensor technology: adoption, policies, retention, audits, data sharing, and contract decisions.

Municipal governanceOct. 20–21

Sterling Heights renews Flock for two years, 4-3, with written limits on searches and sharing

Fact. Sterling Heights City Council voted 4-3 to continue its agreement with Flock Group for plate-reader access. The city’s meeting synopsis records Council Members Yanez, Ziarko, Mijac and Schmidt voting yes and Sierawski, Taylor and Radtke voting no. The vote came at about 12:30 a.m. on October 21, after more than three hours of public comment (WXYZ and Detroit News reporting). The agreement runs 24 months, from December 14, 2026 to December 13, 2028, as the police department and city administration recommended. The city pays $3,000 per camera deployed, from state forfeiture funds; the annual fee reaches $84,100 once all 28 planned readers are in service. The department’s agenda statement says the city has 23 active cameras at 19 locations, stores plate data for up to 30 days, records every login and search and audits them monthly, and has revised its general order to prohibit searches related to immigration enforcement and reproductive health care. Under a Michigan-only sharing policy, only Michigan law-enforcement agencies may view the city’s data — 180 do now, each required to sign a compliance agreement — and federal agencies have no access. The city may end the agreement at any time for convenience, with a $500 removal fee per unit. At the meeting, the police chief said the readers had helped solve 32 cases since August 22 and that the department does not share data with federal agencies or with agencies that have a 287(g) immigration-enforcement agreement; a city spokesperson put the program’s cost to date at about $239,000 (Detroit News reporting).

NCI analysis. Sterling Heights gives the other answer to the questions behind this fall’s exits: keep the system and write the controls into policy — retention, audit frequency, prohibited search purposes and who may see the data. St. Clair Shores is the test case for that approach: there a council member said, and the police chief confirmed, that the FBI had been accessing the city’s data despite assurances that federal agencies would not. A written no-federal-access rule is only as good as the vendor’s sharing settings and the audit that confirms them.

Municipal governanceOct. 11

Madison Heights will disconnect its plate readers after Flock does not supply assurances

Fact. In a notice posted October 11, the City of Madison Heights said it has made “the administrative decision” to disconnect its automated license-plate reader cameras and not renew its Flock contract when it expires. The city says it operated three cameras and had held off on a final decision while waiting for Flock “to provide City staff with assurances that appropriate guardrails were in place” and pending a department directive on use, but that “Flock has been unable to address our concerns or provide those assurances.” No new data has been collected since the cameras were bagged and taken out of active use on September 23. The notice thanks City Council for its input and residents and businesses for their feedback, but records a staff decision rather than a council vote. It also notes that several businesses and organizations in the city contract with Flock independently and that those cameras are privately operated and not managed by the city. Neither the notice nor the news coverage NCI reviewed includes a response from Flock, and the notice does not give the contract’s expiration date.

NCI analysis. Milford also cited the vendor, but on service: downed cameras and responsiveness. Madison Heights’ reason is governance: staff asked for assurances that guardrails were in place and did not get them, and the city put that on the record. That is a procurement step other cities can copy — ask for the controls in writing before renewal and record the answer. The note about privately contracted cameras marks the limit of the decision: ending a city contract does not reach systems the city never controlled.

Municipal governanceAug. 24–Oct. 22

Michigan’s plate-reader decisions this fall, and the bills still in committee

Fact. At least eight Michigan communities have moved to pause or end Flock license-plate cameras since August; one is expanding and one has renewed with written limits. Lansing City Council adopted a non-binding 7-1 resolution on August 24 asking the administration and police to end the city’s Flock contracts, which remain an administration decision. Independence Township’s board voted September 8 to end its agreement, remove all eight cameras and delete the township’s data (FOX 2 reporting). Eastpointe voted 4-1 on September 15 to end its contract after police substantiated misuse by two employees. In St. Clair Shores, the mayor stated a council directive on September 21 to cover the cameras for six months, with the item returning in March 2027; at the same meeting the police chief confirmed that the FBI had been accessing the city’s data for two months, according to the proposed minutes. On September 28, South Lyon’s council voted 5-2 not to renew and to cover the lenses (Local 4 reporting), and Lincoln Park’s council unanimously adopted a resolution to shroud its cameras and evaluate whether to keep replacing damaged ones and what the agreement requires, according to its minutes. Milford’s village council voted 7-0 on October 5 not to renew its remaining 10 cameras. Madison Heights announced on October 11 a staff decision to disconnect its three cameras after Flock did not supply assurances about guardrails. Inkster announced on October 13 that it is expanding from six readers to 20, and Sterling Heights renewed its agreement for two years in a 4-3 vote early on October 21 (city meeting synopsis). Separately, the Oakland County Sheriff’s Office is investigating a former deputy’s alleged searches of the county system (CBS Detroit reporting). At the state level, HB 5492-5493 (guidelines for plate-reader use, introduced January 29), HB 6411-6413 (regulation, penalties and sentencing) and HB 6346 (sharing of captured plate data), both introduced September 24, and SB 1131 (guidelines for plate-reader use, introduced July 29) remain in committee; none was on a posted committee agenda as of October 22.

NCI analysis. The stated reasons map to different controls: council direction over an administrative contract (Lansing), internal misuse and audit (Eastpointe and Oakland County), data sharing beyond what officials were told (St. Clair Shores), equipment and service performance (Milford and Lincoln Park) and documented safeguards from the vendor (Madison Heights). Inkster and Sterling Heights answer the same questions the other way, with written policies and audits. None of these decisions came from a state rule. Michigan has no statute specific to plate readers — the House and Senate bills that would create one are in committee — so contract terms and local policy do most of the governing, and they lapse when a contract does.

Federal legislationSept. 30–Oct. 15

Four federal proposals would restrict license-plate readers, one from a Michigan member

Fact. Rep. Rashida Tlaib of Michigan introduced H.R. 10807, the Get the Flock Out of Our Communities Act, on October 13 with six cosponsors, and presented it at an event in Inkster on October 14. It was referred to the Oversight and Government Reform and Judiciary committees. It would bar federal funds from being used to buy, operate or access automated mass-surveillance systems, including license-plate readers, biometric surveillance and facial recognition; bar federal agencies from using such systems or their data; require systems bought with federal funds to be removed within 30 days of enactment and the data deleted within seven; make data obtained in violation inadmissible; and require states and localities applying for Byrne or COPS law-enforcement grants to certify that they do not operate such systems. On October 15, Rep. Pat Harrigan of North Carolina introduced H.R. 10822 “to restrict the usage of automated license plate reader system by governmental entities”; its text was not yet posted. Earlier, Rep. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez introduced H.R. 10691, the Ban Flock Act, on October 1, and Sens. Bernie Sanders and Jeff Merkley released Senate text on October 2. It would bar federal agencies from acquiring or using automated license-plate readers or their data without explicit authorization from Congress, withhold grants from certain federal agencies from state and local governments that use the readers, and bar federal funds for buying them. On September 30, Sen. Josh Hawley said he will introduce a Stop Flock Abuse Act that would instead set conditions on use: written approval for each search, audit logs, deletion after 10 days except for active criminal investigations, and no sale or sharing of data with non-governmental parties. NCI found no Senate bill number for either Senate proposal in federal bill-status data through October 15. None of the four is law.

NCI analysis. The proposals span the options Michigan’s own bills raise: end the technology’s use, as H.R. 10807 would for federal money and grant recipients; bar federal use, as the Ban Flock Act would; or regulate use through search authorization, retention limits, audits and sharing rules, as Sen. Hawley proposes. H.R. 10807’s grant certification would reach local contracts whatever paid for them, because it conditions Byrne and COPS grants on a state or locality not operating these systems at all.

Policing technologyOct. 2–9

AI-drafted police reports reach Michigan departments; Wayne County’s prosecutor urges caution

Fact. Capital News Service reported on October 2, in a story carried by WCMU on October 9, that several Michigan police departments use Axon’s Draft One, which drafts an officer’s report from body-camera audio. Portage Assistant Police Chief Mike Collier said it saves his department hours and makes reports more accurate because officers narrate observations on scene. Ron Wiles, executive director of the Michigan Association of Chiefs of Police, said the software makes errors, officers must vet every report, and departments should treat it as a tool rather than an “end all, be all.” Wayne County Prosecutor Kym Worthy wrote to county police chiefs that AI “can make errors that jeopardize cases in court” and is developing a policy for AI use by police and public-safety departments in the county; neither the letter nor the policy has been published. A separate WXYZ investigation named Dearborn Heights, which adopted the tool earlier this year without requiring its use, Warren, which began a 60-day pilot in August, and the Kent County Sheriff’s Office, which limits AI-assisted reports to non-violent offenses, and reported that Southfield’s chief opposes adopting it. A 2024 University of South Carolina study found Draft One did not change how long a report took to write; a 2025 follow-up found officers and supervisors reported improvements in time and quality.

NCI analysis. A police report is the document a prosecution is built on, so drafting it by software moves an accuracy question into the evidentiary chain. The Atlas questions here are narrow and answerable from records: what an officer certifies when signing, whether the machine draft and the officer’s edits are retained and disclosed, and what a defendant is told. California answered those questions by statute in 2025: SB 524 requires AI-assisted reports to say so, the first draft to be kept for as long as the final report, and a record of who used the tool and what footage it drew on. NCI found no Michigan bill on the subject, so prosecutors’ and departments’ own policies are the rules that exist; Wayne County’s, once published, can be compared with California’s on those three points.

Prediction-market jurisdictionOct. 9–19

CFTC rule keeping sportsbook wagers out of “swaps” takes effect; event-contract proposal opens for comment

Fact. The two actions the Commodity Futures Trading Commission announced on October 9 were published in the Federal Register on October 19. The interim final rule (91 FR 65969) took effect that day. Under it, a product is not a swap if it is offered by a person licensed to offer gambling under state law, or under tribal law pursuant to the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act, acting within that license; is regulated as gaming under that law; and is not traded on an exchange or other multilateral market. The rule names sports wagers accepted by a licensed sportsbook, casino games, and other licensed games of chance such as bingo, pool wagering, lotteries and raffles. Exchange-traded sports event contracts fall outside the exclusion, and the rule’s preamble cites the Sixth Circuit’s September 25 decision in KalshiEX v. Schuler as one of the rulings that diverged from the statute’s plain text. The proposed rule (91 FR 66022) would add “event contracts, including those based on sports-, politics-, cultural, and weather-related events” to the products the swap definition expressly includes. Comments on both are due November 18. Separately, KalshiEX’s October 9 petition asking the full Sixth Circuit to rehear the September 25 decision in its Ohio and Tennessee cases (Nos. 26-3196 and 26-5235) argues that the two rulemakings “substantially undermine the panel’s holding concerning the meaning of ‘swap.’” As of October 20, the publicly mirrored docket showed no order on the petition.

NCI analysis. The rule now in effect draws the line the Michigan cases turn on: a wager placed with a state-licensed sportsbook is not a swap and stays with state regulators such as the Michigan Gaming Control Board, while a similar contract traded on an exchange is outside that exclusion. If the proposal is finalized, the CFTC would be stating by regulation that exchange-traded sports contracts are swaps — the reading the Sixth Circuit rejected in a decision that binds federal courts in Michigan. How much weight a court gives a later agency rule against a circuit precedent is the open question, and the rehearing petition puts it before the Sixth Circuit first.

In many cases, according to the release, apprenticeship completers would need only a semester of community college courses to finish an associate degree. The program was launched with the state’s skilled building trades unions, MiLEAP, the community college association and the Michigan Workforce Development Board.

NCI analysis. Credit for apprenticeship turns a training credential into a step toward a degree, the kind of skilled pathway BuildReady is built around. The release gives no credit totals for any trade and no count of apprentices who have used the credits, and its “one semester” figure is not tied to specific programs. The curriculum evaluations under way will set those numbers; until they are published, the program’s reach can be described but not measured.

NCI analysis. The four rankings mix three measures — employment after services, apprenticeship counts and credential attainment — against two comparison groups, an unnamed region and the nation, and none is tied to a published table. Civil rights guidanceOct. 23

Federal agencies say seven joint civil-rights guidance documents for schools are not enforceable

Fact. In a notice published in the Federal Register on October 23 (91 FR 66840), the U.S. Department of Justice and Department of Education said seven guidance documents they issued jointly from 2015 to 2023 are not enforceable and that they will not rely on them in enforcing Title IV and Title VI of the Civil Rights Act, the Equal Educational Opportunities Act, the Americans with Disabilities Act and Section 504. The documents are the January 2015 Dear Colleague letter, fact sheet and parent information on English learner students and parents with limited English proficiency; a 2021 resource on COVID-19-related harassment in schools; a 2023 resource on racial discrimination in student discipline; and two 2023 question-and-answer documents on the Supreme Court’s Students for Fair Admissions decision. The agencies said they plan to revisit the documents in light of Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo. The statutes and regulations themselves are unchanged.

NCI analysis. The 2015 English-learner guidance was the most detailed federal description of what districts must do to give English learners meaningful access to instruction and to communicate with parents in a language they understand. The underlying duties come from the statutes and court decisions, which this notice does not change. What changes is the federal reference point districts and families used to judge compliance, which leaves more of that interpretation to state guidance and to courts.

Student attendanceOct. 13

Special educationComments closed Oct. 16

Proposed special-education rules would change state-complaint procedures and add a cross-category endorsement

Fact. The Department of Education’s Office of Special Education held public hearings on October 12 and 13 on Rule Set 2026-14 ED, proposed amendments to Michigan’s special-education rules published in the Michigan Register on September 15; written comments closed at 5 p.m. on October 16. The proposal would align rule timelines with state law; remove private entities from the definition of “agency” to match federal regulation; address a district’s notice obligations when it will not conduct a requested evaluation and after any IEP team meeting; remove language allowing a state complaint to be delayed for informal resolution; delete a provision allowing “stay put” of a student’s placement during a state complaint; and add a stand-alone special-education teacher endorsement allowing an educator to be placed in programs across multiple disability areas. MDE says the rules would take effect immediately after filing with the Secretary of State.

NCI analysis. Two of the changes alter how a family’s state complaint proceeds: it could no longer be paused for informal resolution, and a student’s placement would no longer be held in place while it is decided. MDE describes both as alignments with federal regulations (34 CFR 300.152 and 300.518); stay-put protection during due-process hearings comes from federal regulation and is not part of this change. The cross-category endorsement would let one teacher be placed across disability areas; its preparation requirements, compared with single-category endorsements, are the detail in the rule text that determines what it means for students.
