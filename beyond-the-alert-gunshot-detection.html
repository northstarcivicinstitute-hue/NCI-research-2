Beyond the Alert

A practical framework for judging gunshot-detection technology by alerts, workload, victim aid, investigations, community effects and cost.

Published September 2026Updated September 19, 2026Author Donavan NormanReading time 8 min
Gunshot DetectionPublic SafetyEvaluationProcurementDetroit
Download PDFDownload Word fileAll briefs
NCI analysis

NCI does not treat gunshot detection as inherently successful or unsuccessful. The relevant question is what a local program adds after the alert enters the system: victim aid, evidence, investigative progress, violence reduction, workload, community effects, and comparative value. Renewal should require local evidence across that full chain.

Plain-language terms used in this brief
Acoustic gunshot detection
A sensor and software system that identifies sounds likely to be outdoor gunfire and sends an alert to a public-safety agency.
Alert-to-outcome tracking
Following an alert through dispatch, response, victim or evidence recovery, investigation and final result.
Clearance rate
The share of reported crimes that police clear by arrest or another accepted method.
Incremental alert
An event the system identifies that ordinary 911 reporting probably would not have produced.

What the latest evidence changes

The strongest new evidence is not a single city result. In February 2026, the first systematic review and meta-analysis focused on acoustic gunshot detection and crime identified 14 studies and analyzed 44 effect sizes from 8 studies with sufficient information. The pooled rate-ratio estimate was 1.02, with a 95% confidence interval from 0.90 to 1.16, indicating no statistically significant overall relationship between deployment and crime. The authors also found substantial between-study heterogeneity. [1]

Three months later, a peer-reviewed Detroit study reported a 6.5% reduction in violent crime in treated areas. That estimate is important because it differs from much prior literature, but it is also statistically suggestive rather than definitive: the wild-bootstrap p-value was 0.074. The same study found an 8.5% increase in recorded nonviolent crime, a 322% increase in gunshot-related calls, a 10.4% decline in non-gunshot Priority 1 calls, no significant change in clearance rates, and no conclusive travel-time difference. [2]

Evidence standard

The meta-analysis and Detroit study should be read together. The pooled evidence does not show a general crime-reduction effect; Detroit suggests that effects can differ by local implementation and context. Neither finding substitutes for a jurisdiction's own outcome, workload, cost, and data-quality evidence.

Measure five separate outcomes

Across Denver, Milwaukee, and Richmond, an NIJ-supported study found roughly twice as many shooting notifications and two to three times as much staff time after implementation. Response was generally faster for technology alerts, but the time advantage largely occurred between notification and officer assignment rather than during officer travel. [3]

Detroit is a useful test, not a national verdict

The Detroit study used a staggered rollout. Areas activated in March 2021 were compared with areas not receiving coverage until October 2022, using police data from January 2017 through October 2022. The design strengthens the analysis, but the period ends before later expansions, operational changes, and current contracts. [2]

Track what happens after each alert

Durham's 12-month, three-square-mile pilot illustrates how raw alerts narrow as the question becomes more consequential. Researchers counted 1,447 total notifications: 57% technology-only, 15% both technology and 911, and 28% 911-only. Among 282 confirmed gunshot events, the distribution changed to 26% technology-only, 34% both, and 40% 911-only. [4]

The pilot recorded 52 serious incidents involving a wound or death. The system alerted for 26, while 911 calls were received for 50. Eight of the missed alerts were attributed to human or system error. Technology-only notifications led to about 2.3 additional deployments per day, seven additional incidents resulting in arrest, and 71 additional evidence-collection incidents. Median response time fell by about 1.2 minutes in the pilot area, but the researchers could not determine that the program reduced gun violence. [4]

Reliable evaluation starts with reliable data

A city cannot evaluate a program if alerts are not consistently coded, corrected, and linked to downstream records. Contract language and agency procedure should establish the record before deployment.

  • Use a unique CAD/event code for technology alerts and preserve links to 911 calls, dispatch, incident reports, evidence, EMS, and case records.
  • Record the initial alert classification, any human review, later correction, and reason for reclassification without overwriting the original record.
  • Define how false alerts, missed incidents, duplicate notifications, indoor gunfire, and events inside vehicles will be handled in performance reporting.
  • Preserve the audio, location, analyst notes, version information, and audit logs needed for discovery, evidence review, complaint investigation, and independent evaluation, subject to lawful retention limits.
  • Require agency access to event-level data and prohibit contract terms that give a vendor veto over public reporting or unfavorable evaluation findings.
  • Document changes in coverage, thresholds, software, review practices, dispatch priority, staffing, and response protocols so apparent outcome changes can be interpreted.

Chicago research published in 2025 did not identify a clear additional racial disparity in arrests or stops beyond patterns associated with standard gunfire calls, but it also showed that both alert pathways were linked to enforcement activity and geography. Community effects therefore need local measurement rather than assumption. [7]

Victim discovery and medical response deserve separate measurement

Victim aid can be a legitimate benefit even when crime and clearance effects are uncertain. Camden research examined 267 gunshot-wound cases and reported faster police response and transport than EMS in the observed setting. The study was retrospective and single-center, so it does not establish that the same result will occur elsewhere. [6]

A local evaluation should distinguish alerts that only generated a deployment from alerts that materially changed victim discovery, first aid, EMS activation, transport, or time to treatment. These outcomes should not be inferred from alert volume.

A five-question renewal test

  1. Incremental information: What events did the system identify that ordinary 911 reporting probably would have missed?
  2. Conversion: What share of incremental alerts produced victim aid, evidence, a usable lead, a recovered firearm, an arrest, a clearance, or another documented result?
  3. Net system effect: After staffing, unproductive deployments, false or duplicate alerts, and effects on other priority demand, what improved?
  4. Legitimacy and distribution: Where were alerts and enforcement concentrated, what complaints or disparities appeared, and what did community feedback show?
  5. Comparative value: Would the same money and personnel plausibly produce more safety through another intervention or a different deployment design?

The decision memo should state whether the jurisdiction will continue, modify, narrow, expand, or exit, and tie the decision to metrics defined before the review period. Renewal should be affirmative rather than automatic.

NIJ's technology-adoption guide begins with problem analysis and separates technical, operational, and governance questions. That structure is useful here: a reliable sensor is not the same thing as a successful program. [9]

A practical Michigan reporting standard

  • A one-page public program profile: coverage, contract term, full annual cost, retention, sharing, dispatch priority, and evaluation design.
  • A quarterly alert funnel: raw notifications, confirmed incidents, productive responses, case outcomes, victim-aid outcomes, and corrections.
  • Workload and opportunity cost: units, officer and analyst hours, effects on unrelated high-priority demand, and overtime or staffing needs.
  • Community and distribution measures: geography, complaints, enforcement activity, and structured resident feedback.
  • A public renewal memorandum using the five-question test and comparing credible alternatives.

Durham's separate sentiment study used nine focus groups and two interviews with 30 residents and found skepticism among many participants. That sample is not representative of every community, but it demonstrates that public experience should be measured rather than treated as an afterthought. [8]

Bottom line

The latest research strengthens the case for disciplined local evaluation. The 2026 meta-analysis does not show a general crime-reduction effect, while Detroit reports a context-specific violent-crime estimate alongside a much larger increase in gunshot-related calls and no detected clearance improvement. The program decision should turn on local conversion of alerts into useful outcomes, full operating cost, data quality, community effects, and comparative value.
